Monsoon cloudbursts can fill a street in minutes.
Yet most warnings only tell you that it will rain.
Drishti tells you where the water will stand, three hours before it does.
This is Smart India Hackathon problem statement 26085, from the Ministry of Earth Sciences:
urban flood nowcasting.
City forecasts stop at the district.
But a campus floods one low corner at a time.
The lane behind a hostel.
The underpass by the main gate.
The drain inlet that has been silting up since June.
An operator on the ground needs five answers.
Where will it flood?
When?
How deep, and for how long?
Why?
And what should we do about it?
Our answer is Drishti: a flood digital twin of the KIET campus in Ghaziabad.
It models the campus on a 5-metre grid, with real terrain, real buildings, and a drain network.
Every five minutes, it runs one loop.
It reads the sky, from radar and 8 rain gauges, checking every reading for spikes, frozen
sensors and gaps.
It nowcasts the rain with a 20-member ensemble.
It updates the twin, routing water over the ground and through the pipes.
A U-Net, trained on 290 physics runs, turns every rain member into a depth map.
And hotspots get a full physics re-run.
One complete cycle takes about 22 seconds, on a laptop.
So what makes Drishti different?
First, physics and AI together: a mass-conserving model of the drains and the surface, with a
fast neural surrogate on top.
Second, it is probabilistic.
Every forecast carries its spread, and the chance of crossing 10, 20 or 30 cm.
Third, it closes the loop.
Live sensor readings correct the twin, and expose blocked drains.
And fourth, it explains its reasoning, and turns forecasts into decisions.
Let's see the prototype.
The homepage replays a simulated squall over the real campus terrain in 3D.
Rain falls, drains fill and surcharge, and water rises in the low corners.
The operations dashboard has eight views.
Situation shows the twin as it stands right now: forecast health, rainfall, observation quality,
and live alerts.
Forecast plays the depth map from now to three hours ahead.
Switch between the expected depth, P10, P50, P90, and the peak.
Click any street cell, and Drishti forecasts that exact spot: all 20-members, when the threshold
is crossed, and a why panel that weighs rainfall, runoff, terrain, drainage and asset faults, to
name the main driver.
Hotspots maps the chance of exceeding 10, 20 or 30 cm, lists an early warning for every hotspot
tile, and shows the radar ensemble behind it.
And if the radar fails?
The outage drill switches to the real degraded forecast.
Gauges only, uncertainty widened by half, and a clear degraded banner, so no one trusts it more
than they should.
Drainage is the closed loop.
Nine virtual sensors watch the network.
Folding their readings in cuts water level error by 87%.
The residuals point to the likely cause, a blocked pipe or a stuck sensor, with a work order
ready to raise.
Roads and routes turns depth into impact.
Every road is scored for two-wheelers, cars, buses, ambulances and fire trucks.
The planner finds three routes that avoid the streets that will be flooded by the time you reach
them, and checks access to every building.
The action lab runs full physics what-ifs.
Heavier rain.
Drains at half capacity.
A pump failure.
A rising nala.
It ranks interventions by how much flooding they prevent and what they cost, and nothing runs
until a person approves it.
Validation scores every cycle against the reference run.
And the system view shows the loop timings, a versioned API, Common Alerting Protocol alerts,
and role-based access for every kind of user.
The results.
Three hours ahead, on storms it never saw, the fast model scores a critical success index of
0.79, against 0.24 for persistence.
67% of the cells that later flooded were flagged in advance, a median 20 minutes early, with
just 2% false alarms.
And we are honest about it.
Every number is labelled observed, derived, modelled, or synthetic.
The drainage is synthetic, the scores come from a twin experiment, and the ensemble is still too
confident.
That is what we fix next.
